Research agenda

When is an AI system justified in making a claim about the state of a collective?

A classroom can be busy with discussion while struggling to reach a shared understanding. My research asks when patterns of interaction justify an inference about a collective condition—and how that inference should be tested.

The problem

From observable activity to a justified interpretation.

We can count contributions, measure participation, and summarize individual responses. But when do those observations support a claim that a group is confused, aligned, engaged, or in conflict?

Collective-State Inference (CSI) investigates that step from evidence to interpretation. It develops a computational organization theory specifying when an AI output may be interpreted as an estimate of a latent collective condition: something inferred from evidence rather than observed directly.

Sometimes an aggregate measure is appropriate. In other cases, the relationships among participants and the sequence of their interactions may be essential. The research asks which representation fits the condition being studied, and whether it adds value beyond simpler alternatives.

A classroom example

Could confusion emerge through classroom interaction?

Hypothetical illustration. This example explains the research question; it is not an implemented or validated CSI application.

Imagine students working through a shared problem during a lesson. The participating students, their shared task, and the observation period define a bounded collective for this example.

Students repeatedly seek clarification from one another. Conflicting interpretations circulate, proposed explanations fail to resolve disagreements, and the same misunderstandings reappear across exchanges.

These patterns suggest a candidate emergent state of collective confusion: a difficulty in establishing shared understanding that develops through interaction. That interpretation does not imply that every student is confused.

Two classrooms could ask the same number of questions and report similar average confidence. In one, explanations resolve uncertainty; in the other, misunderstandings persist and reinforce one another. Counts alone may miss that difference.

Yet questions and disagreement can also signal productive exploration. To justify a confusion estimate, a study would need to define the condition, distinguish those alternatives, compare interaction-based models with simpler measures, and test estimates against independent evidence of shared understanding.

A potential educational use would be to help an instructor decide when to check understanding or revisit an explanation. Whether such support improves teaching would require separate evaluation. Any study would also need appropriate consent, privacy safeguards, and limits on individual attribution.

Research questions

What would make the inference defensible?

  1. Define the collective and its condition. Who belongs, over what period, and what makes the proposed state a property of the collective?
  2. Connect evidence to meaning. When are individual summaries sufficient, and when do relationships, context, or interaction history matter?
  3. Test competing explanations. Can an estimate distinguish confusion from exploration, and outperform simpler comparison models?
  4. Represent uncertainty and limits. When should a system qualify or withhold an estimate, and how can people question its interpretation?

If simpler measures explain the condition equally well, richer representations are not justified for that case.

Current status. Collective-State Inference: A Computational Organization Theory for Collective-Aware Artificial Intelligence is unpublished and currently in external journal peer review following editorial suitability assessment. It has not been accepted, and CSI has not yet been empirically validated.

Why I study this

A question that grew from building systems.

CSI's initial inspiration emerged during the conception and development of Vibe Village, a social-music platform centered on shared listening and participation. That work prompted a broader question: what can observable interaction tell us about a collective, and where do the limits of that interpretation lie?

I bring that question to research through a background in enterprise architecture, distributed systems, and observability.
